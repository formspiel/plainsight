Donut / Pie Chart

A circle divided into wedges, each proportional to its share of the whole — angle (and area) encoding, not position or length. Good for a small number of categories where the reader's main question is "how does each part compare to the whole," not "what's the exact trend" or "how do these compare to each other in detail."

One year of fictional insurance claims, by category — a single part-to-whole snapshot, not a time series like the two bar charts. Tab into the chart, then use the arrow keys described below.

Long description ▾

Auto Collision is by far the largest category, at 210 of 600 total claims (35%) — more than the next two categories combined. Weather & Storm Damage is second at 130 claims (22%), followed by Water Damage (Non-Weather) at 95 (16%) and Liability Claims at 78 (13%). Theft & Vandalism and Wildlife & Unusual Incidents are the two smallest categories, at 52 (9%) and 35 (6%) respectively — together barely more than a third of Auto Collision alone.

When to use it

Use a donut or pie chart when there's a small number of categories (roughly 2–6) that genuinely sum to one meaningful whole, and the reader's main question is about each part's share of that whole — "what fraction of total claims is Auto Collision?" — not exact values or trends over time.

It's the wrong choice when:

  • There are more than about 6–7 categories. This is a harder ceiling than the Stacked Bar Chart's: a stacked bar's segments each still have their own edge-to-edge height to compare, but a pie/donut's wedges only have angle, and human angle-judgment gets unreliable fast once wedges get thin and numerous. This demo's own six categories are close to that ceiling already — a real "insurance claims by sub-type" breakdown with 15–20 categories would be a textbook case for "should this even be a chart," not a design problem to solve with smaller labels.
  • Readers need to compare two specific slices precisely (e.g. "is Weather & Storm Damage more or less than double Water Damage?"). Angle comparison between two non-adjacent wedges is genuinely harder for the eye than comparing two bars against a shared baseline — a bar chart communicates the same underlying numbers more reliably for this specific question, even though it loses the "this is a whole made of parts" framing a donut gives for free.
  • The categories don't actually sum to a meaningful 100%. A common misuse: plotting a survey question where respondents could pick multiple options, so the "whole" the slices are supposed to add up to isn't real. If the underlying total isn't meaningful, no amount of accessible wiring fixes the chart choice itself.
  • Readers need to track this same breakdown changing over time. A stacked bar chart (this site's first chart type) shows the same part-to-whole relationship at every point along a time axis, which a single donut snapshot can't.

Interaction complexity tier

Exploratory. A reader can navigate to and get the exact value and percentage of every individual category, not just an overall chart summary. With only 6 categories this chart is far smaller than the two bar charts, but the same reasoning applies: a single role="img" summary would still strand keyboard and screen reader users below what a sighted user gets from looking at the chart, so this demo implements full point-by-point navigation here too.

Expected accessibility tree

Same top-level pattern as the bar charts: the visual <svg aria-hidden="true"> is purely decorative, and all accessibility semantics live in a parallel layer of real, focusable <button> elements — one per wedge. What's different here: each button's actual clickable area is clipped (via CSS clip-path) to match its wedge's exact shape, not a rectangle — see "Common failure patterns" below for why a plain rectangular hit area doesn't work well for wedge-shaped targets.

Keyboard pattern

The whole chart is a single Tab stop, same as both bar charts. Unlike the Stacked Bar Chart, there's no hierarchy to move through — every wedge sits at the same level, so Left/Right alone cover the whole chart (closer to the Double Bar Graph's flatter model than the Stacked Bar Chart's bar-summary-plus-segment one).

Legend as a filter

Same idiom as both bar charts (see the Stacked Bar Chart's own "Legend as a filter" for the fuller rationale), applied to angle instead of bar height: hiding a category recomputes every remaining wedge's angle from scratch, so they expand to fill the freed-up 360° — the same "re-stack" idea, just around a ring instead of up a column. The hidden category's wedge and hotspot are removed from the DOM entirely, the center total updates to match what's actually shown, and the one category remaining visible (if everything else is hidden) can't be turned off, locked via aria-disabled rather than disappearing from the tab order. The data table fallback is deliberately not affected by this filter, same as both bar charts. This is one implementation of the general Legends as Filters cross-cutting pattern.

Screen reader behavior

Because every navigable stop is a real, focusable <button>, moving focus is enough to trigger a standard AT announcement — role, name, and nothing else, the same reasoning as both bar charts. The one-time instructions are attached via aria-describedby and are also visible on screen (see the hint above the chart), not just exposed to AT.

As with both bar charts, toggling a legend entry doesn't move focus anywhere, so the visually-hidden aria-live="polite" region is what carries the news that a category was hidden or shown (and, implicitly, that every remaining category's percentage just changed too).

Common failure patterns seen in audits

Seeded with well-known, representative failures to start this page — not yet a team-sourced catalog from real audits. Replace/extend as the team encounters real examples (see CLAUDE.md's sourcing note).

  • A rectangular (or worse, invisible-centroid-dot) hit target for a wedge-shaped slice. A plain rectangle sized to a wedge's bounding box either overlaps into neighboring slices (if sized generously) or leaves real parts of a large wedge unclickable (if sized conservatively) — and a small fixed-size dot at each wedge's centroid has the opposite problem: too small for a large slice's generous visual area, and prone to sitting outside a very thin slice's actual bounds entirely. This demo instead clips each hotspot's hit area to the exact same arc path the visible wedge is drawn with (see "Expected accessibility tree" above) — worth checking for specifically any time a chart's interactive targets are anything other than a plain rectangle. (WCAG 2.5.5 Target Size is about size, not shape, but a mismatched hit shape is a closely related, easy-to-miss usability failure with no SC of its own.)
  • A contrast palette computed for a linear list, reused unchecked on a ring. Found while building this very demo: a 5-color high-contrast palette already computed so every adjacent pair clears 3:1 in a stacked bar (where the first and last segments are never actually next to each other) turned out to fail badly — 1.09:1 — on the one pair a ring adds that a stack never has: the wrap between the last slice and the first. Fixed here by choosing where the new sixth category sits in the ring specifically to avoid that pair, not by patching the color values themselves (see tokens.css's own comment on --series-6). Worth checking for specifically any time an existing categorical palette gets reused on a new chart shape. (WCAG 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast)
  • Too many slices, full stop. Unlike a stacked bar (which degrades gracefully to a legend/keyboard-navigation slog past 5–6 segments, see that page's own "When to use it"), a pie/donut's core visual promise — judge each part's share at a glance — breaks down well before that, once wedges get thin enough that angle-judgment itself becomes unreliable for every user, sighted or not. No amount of accessible wiring fixes a chart type that was the wrong choice for the data (see "When to use it" above). (Not a single WCAG SC — this is a chart-choice problem accessible engineering can't solve after the fact.)
  • Segments distinguished by color alone, with no pattern/texture and no text alternative — fails for colorblind users and anyone in high-contrast/grayscale contexts, same as every chart on this site. (WCAG 1.4.1 Use of Color)
  • Percentage labels shown only on hover, with no keyboard path to them and no equivalent in a data table — a sighted mouse user gets exact shares, everyone else is left estimating wedge angle by eye. This demo shows percentage labels directly on large-enough wedges as a sighted convenience, but the real value lives in each hotspot's own accessible name and the data table regardless of hover. (WCAG 2.1.1 Keyboard, 1.1.1 Non-text Content)
  • A data table fallback exists but isn't a real equivalent — missing column/row headers, not actually reachable via keyboard, or omitting values the chart shows (e.g. each category's percentage share, not just its raw count). (WCAG 1.3.1 Info and Relationships)
  • An interactive legend that only changes color, not keyboard/SR reachability — same shortcut, same fix, as both bar charts (see the Stacked Bar Chart's own "Common failure patterns" for the fuller explanation). (WCAG 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value)

Mobile / touch equivalent

This chart doesn't need the bar charts' horizontal-scroll floor at all: 6 categories stay legible shrunk to a normal responsive width, unlike 30 months of dense bar data, which has a real legibility floor a shrinking viewport blows straight past. The chart canvas simply scales down like an ordinary responsive image, no scroll container involved.

The clip-path-shaped hotspot buttons (see "Common failure patterns" above) work as normal tap targets — modern mobile browsers hit-test a clip-path-clipped element against its clipped shape, not its full box, the same way desktop Chrome/Firefox/Safari do. This is specifically flagged as "not yet verified" in the SR × browser matrix above rather than assumed, since it's a newer, less commonly-relied-on technique than the bar charts' plain rectangular hotspots.

The toolbar's hover/focus tooltip is suppressed on touch, the chart's own data-point tooltip is not — identical reasoning and identical mechanism to both bar charts (see the Stacked Bar Chart's own "Mobile / touch equivalent" for the full explanation).
