Double Bar Graph

Diverging, mirrored-axis bars — values plotted either side of a shared zero line, rather than all anchored to a common edge. Good for two opposing counts per category, where seeing both the size of each side and the balance between them matters.

Long description ▾

Approved claims hold fairly steady all through 2023, between about 41 and 50 a month, with denials low and flat at 8–12. A stricter review process introduced in January 2024 pushes denials sharply higher — 20–26 a month from January through May 2024, roughly double the normal rate, while approvals dip to 35–39. The process is relaxed starting June 2024: denials fall back to 15, then to single digits by September 2024, and approvals recover to their normal 41–51 range for the rest of the period through June 2025.

See the Stacked Bar Chart for this project's fuller template walkthrough — this page follows the same pattern (accessible hotspot overlay, interactive legend, sortable data table) and mostly calls out what's different for a diverging chart rather than repeating everything.

When to use it

Use a double bar graph when there are exactly two related-but-opposing counts per category, and readers need to see both the size of each side and the balance between them — approved vs. denied, income vs. expenses, this year vs. last year.

It's the wrong choice when:

  • There are more than two series. A diverging axis only reads cleanly with one value on each side — for three or more categories per group, a grouped/clustered bar chart (values side by side, not mirrored) serves better.
  • The two values aren't naturally opposed or comparable on the same scale (e.g. one is a count and the other is a percentage). Mirroring them around a shared zero line implies a relationship that isn't actually there.
  • Readers mainly need the combined total of both sides (e.g. "how many claims were decided this month, period"). Nothing on this chart shows that sum directly — see "Expected accessibility tree" below for how the accessible version handles this instead.

Interaction complexity tier

Exploratory. A reader can navigate to and get the exact value of every Approved and Denied bar, not just an overall chart summary. With 30 months × 2 series (60 data points), a single role="img" summary would strand keyboard and screen reader users well below what a sighted user gets by looking at the chart — so this demo implements full point-by-point navigation, the same reasoning as the Stacked Bar Chart.

Expected accessibility tree

Same overall pattern as the Stacked Bar Chart: the visual <svg aria-hidden="true"> is purely decorative, and a parallel layer of real, invisibly-styled <button> elements carries all accessibility semantics. The difference is the hotspot model itself — there's no "bar summary" stop here, because Approved and Denied aren't parts of a whole the way stacked segments are. Each one gets its own hotspot text stating both its own value and what share of that month's decided claims (approved + denied) it represents — the closest equivalent to the Stacked Bar Chart's percentage-of-total, computed from the two values that exist rather than a separate combined total hotspot that would otherwise be unreachable by mouse/touch (see that page's "Common failure patterns" for why that specific shortcut doesn't work).

Keyboard pattern

The whole chart is a single Tab stop, same roving-tabindex approach as the Stacked Bar Chart. The model is flatter here, though: there's no bar-summary-to-segment hierarchy to move through, just two fixed stops per month, so Up and Down jump directly to Approved or Denied rather than climbing a stack — and there's no Escape row, because there's no nested level to back out of.

Legend as a filter

Same idiom as the Stacked Bar Chart's legend (see that page's "Legend as a filter" for the fuller rationale), simplified: hiding a series here doesn't require recomputing anything about the other one, since Approved and Denied are independent counts, not parts of a stack that has to close a gap. Hiding Approved just removes its bars and hotspots entirely; Denied is completely unaffected. With only two series, hiding either one immediately locks the other (its button gets aria-disabled) — there's no scenario here where more than one toggle is needed to reach that state. The data table fallback is, as with the Stacked Bar Chart, deliberately not affected by this filter.

Screen reader behavior

Because every navigable stop is a real, focusable <button>, moving focus is enough to trigger a standard AT announcement — role, name, and nothing else, the same reasoning as the Stacked Bar Chart. The one-time instructions are attached via aria-describedby and are also visible on screen (see the hint above the chart), not just exposed to AT.

As with the Stacked Bar Chart, toggling a legend entry doesn't move focus anywhere, so the visually-hidden aria-live="polite" region is what carries the news that a series was hidden or shown.

Common failure patterns seen in audits

Seeded with well-known, representative failures to start this page — not yet a team-sourced catalog from real audits. Replace/extend as the team encounters real examples (see CLAUDE.md's sourcing note).

  • Raw signed values read out or labeled on a diverging axis — a chart that shows "-9" for 9 denied claims, whether visually on the axis or announced to a screen reader, forces the reader to do the sign-flip themselves. This demo's axis labels show absolute values (see script.js's Y_TICKS comment), and every hotspot's text is plain language ("Denied: 9 claims") with no sign at all. (WCAG 1.3.1 Info and Relationships — the visual convention and the programmatically exposed value need to agree on what a reader actually needs)
  • Segments distinguished by color alone, with no pattern/texture and no text alternative — same failure as the Stacked Bar Chart, same fix (a toggleable, forced-colors-mandatory pattern fill).
  • No visual distinction for the zero/reference line — if every gridline looks the same, a reader has to count ticks to figure out where "positive" turns into "negative." This demo gives the zero line the same bolder treatment the Stacked Bar Chart gives its (edge) baseline. (WCAG 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast)
  • No keyboard path to individual values — same failure, same fix as the Stacked Bar Chart: a full hotspot overlay, not just a mouse-hover tooltip. (WCAG 2.1.1 Keyboard, 1.1.1 Non-text Content)
  • A data table fallback exists but isn't a real equivalent — missing column/row headers, not actually reachable via keyboard, or omitting values the chart shows. (WCAG 1.3.1 Info and Relationships)
  • Dense charts shrink to illegible size on small screens instead of reflowing — same fix as the Stacked Bar Chart: a legible-size floor and horizontal scroll below it, not indefinite shrinking. (WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow)

Mobile / touch equivalent

Same behavior as the Stacked Bar Chart: below roughly 900px the chart stops shrinking and scrolls horizontally instead, with a gradient-plus-chevron sighted-only signal for "there's more this way." The hotspot buttons work as normal tap targets for sighted touch users and are real focusable elements for switch-access and other assistive input methods. The same two honest caveats apply: VoiceOver (iOS) and TalkBack swipe navigation walk the accessibility tree in document order regardless of tabindex (60 stops here, not 180 — still a real cost, just a smaller one than the Stacked Bar Chart's), and there's no distinct visual "focused/selected" state beyond the default focus ring, which isn't generally shown for touch taps at all.
