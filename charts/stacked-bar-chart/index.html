Stacked Bar Chart

A vertical bar per category, subdivided into segments that sum to the bar's total. Good for showing both a whole (the total) and its composition (the parts) across many categories at once.

Long description ▾

Auto Collision is the largest single category most months, holding fairly steady between about 17 and 27 claims. Weather & Storm Damage swings the most: it spikes to 20–31 claims each spring (March–May) and again each autumn (September–November), then drops below 10 in summer and winter. Water Damage (Non-Weather) shows close to the opposite seasonal pattern — pipe-burst claims spike to 15–23 each winter (December–February) and fall to single digits the rest of the year. Liability Claims stay comparatively flat all year, between 6 and 10. Wildlife & Unusual Incidents stays low most months but more than doubles every October and November, to 8–11 claims, during deer season. Monthly totals range from 44 to 73 claims, with the highest totals concentrated in spring storm season and October/November.

This page is the template the rest of Plainsight's chart pages follow. If you're new here, start with Getting Started — and remember the core thesis: a data table fallback is a genuine parallel option, not a substitute for making the chart itself the accessible experience.

When to use it

Use a stacked bar chart when readers need both the total per category and a sense of how that total breaks down into parts — for example, total insurance claims per month, and which claim categories drove that total.

It's the wrong choice when:

  • Readers mainly need to compare one segment's value across categories (e.g. "was Weather Damage higher in March or September?"). Stacked segments don't share a common baseline except the bottom-most one, so that comparison is genuinely hard to make visually — a grouped/clustered bar chart serves it better.
  • There are more than 5–6 segments. Beyond that, both the visual legend and the segment-level keyboard/SR navigation described below become a slog — consider splitting into small multiples instead.
  • Only two categories are being compared. A simpler diverging bar (see the upcoming Double Bar Graph page) usually communicates that more directly.

Interaction complexity tier

Exploratory. A reader can navigate to and get the exact value of every individual segment, not just an overall chart summary. With 30 months × 5 categories (150 data points) behind this chart, a single role="img" summary would strand keyboard and screen reader users well below what a sighted user gets by looking at the chart — so this demo implements full point-by-point navigation. (A "Summary" tier — one accessible name describing the whole chart, no per-point navigation — is legitimate for genuinely simple charts; it isn't for this one.)

Expected accessibility tree

The visual chart is an <svg aria-hidden="true"> — purely decorative. All accessibility semantics live in a parallel layer of real, invisibly-styled <button> elements positioned exactly over each bar and each segment. This sidesteps inconsistent AT support for ARIA on SVG shapes (see Canvas vs SVG) and means screen readers announce each stop automatically on focus — no custom aria-live wiring needed for basic navigation.

Keyboard pattern

The whole chart is a single Tab stop. Arrow keys move a roving-tabindex cursor within it — this is what keeps 150 data points from becoming 150 Tab stops (see Navigation at scale). "Down" and "Up" move vertically through the visual stack, matching what a sighted user's eye would do.

Keyboard pattern for the stacked bar chart

TabMoves focus into the chart, landing on whichever stop was last focused (the first month's bar summary, initially). Moves focus out entirely on the way back — this is one Tab stop, not many.
Shift+TabMoves focus out of the chart, backward.
Right ArrowMoves to the next month, staying at the same level (bar summary stays on bar summary; a given category stays on that category).
Left ArrowMoves to the previous month, same level.
Down ArrowFrom the bar summary: enters the stack at its topmost visible segment. From a segment: moves down, toward the base. A series hidden via the legend has no stop here at all — there's nothing to skip over, because it was never placed there to begin with (see "Legend as a filter" below).
Up ArrowFrom a segment: moves up, toward the top of the stack of currently-visible segments. From the topmost visible segment: exits back to the bar summary.
HomeJumps to the first month (January 2023), same level.

EscapeFrom a segment, jumps directly back to that month's bar summary.
Tab / Enter or SpaceEach legend entry is its own standalone Tab stop (not part of the chart's roving group) — Enter or Space toggles it, same as any native button.

Legend as a filter

Clicking (or Enter/Space-activating) a legend entry hides that series everywhere: the bar segments close the gap and re-stack, each bar's own total shrinks to the sum of whatever's still visible, and the hidden series' hotspot doesn't exist in the DOM at all — keyboard navigation has nothing to skip over because there's nothing there, not a present-but-excluded stop. A live region (visually hidden) announces what changed on every toggle, since hiding a series changes every other segment's own text without moving focus anywhere to trigger a fresh announcement on its own. The one series remaining visible, if every other one is hidden, can't be turned off — its button gets aria-disabled rather than disappearing from the tab order, so the chart can never end up showing nothing at all. The data table fallback is deliberately not affected by this — it stays a complete, independent view of every category's exact values regardless of what's currently toggled in the chart, per this project's own thesis that the table is a genuine parallel option, not something that should depend on the chart's display state.

Screen reader behavior

Because every navigable stop is a real, focusable <button>, moving focus is enough to trigger a standard AT announcement — role ("button"), name (the full sentence above), and position isn't separately announced by us since we don't use a grid/listbox role that implies set-size/position-in-set. The one-time instructions ("Use Left and Right arrow keys to move between months...") are attached to the chart container via aria-describedby and are announced once, on first entry, not repeated per stop. They're also visible on screen now (see the hint above the chart in the Live demo), not just exposed to AT — a sighted keyboard user has no other way to discover that arrow keys do anything here.

Toggling a legend entry doesn't move focus anywhere (focus stays on the legend button itself), so there's no fresh button-focus announcement to carry the news that every other segment's value/percentage just changed. The visually-hidden aria-live="polite" region (see "Expected accessibility tree" above) exists specifically to cover that gap — it's the one place on this page an announcement is triggered by something other than focus movement.

The floating tooltip that appears on hover and keyboard focus is aria-hidden="true" and announces nothing extra — it exists purely so a sighted mouse or keyboard user sees the same value a screen reader user already hears, not to duplicate that announcement.

Common failure patterns seen in audits

Seeded with well-known, representative failures to start this page — not yet a team-sourced catalog from real audits. Replace/extend as the team encounters real examples (see CLAUDE.md's sourcing note).

  • Segments distinguished by color alone, with no pattern/texture and no text alternative — fails for colorblind users and anyone in high-contrast/grayscale contexts. (WCAG 1.4.1 Use of Color)
  • Adjacent segment colors don't meet non-text contrast. Found while building this very demo: this chart's own default palette measures well under the 3:1 minimum between neighboring stacked segments (as low as 1.14:1 between two of them) — even the 1px separator between segments only reaches 2.36:1 against one of the five colors. prefers-contrast: more swaps in a palette computed to clear 3:1 on every adjacent pair (try it: enable "increase contrast" in your OS/browser settings and reload). Left visible here deliberately rather than quietly fixed, because this is a genuinely common real-world failure, not a hypothetical one. (WCAG 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast)
  • No keyboard path to individual segment values — totals and breakdowns are only revealed by a mouse-hover tooltip. (WCAG 2.1.1 Keyboard, 1.1.1 Non-text Content)
  • Generic or missing accessible names on bars/segments (e.g. "bar 14" with no data), so a screen reader user can tell something is there but not what it means. (WCAG 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value; 1.1.1 Non-text Content)
  • A data table fallback exists but isn't a real equivalent — missing column/row headers, not actually reachable via keyboard, or omitting values the chart shows (e.g. category totals). (WCAG 1.3.1 Info and Relationships)
  • Dense charts shrink to illegible size on small screens instead of reflowing — the whole SVG scaling down with the viewport, rather than keeping bars/labels at a legible minimum size and letting the chart scroll horizontally past that point. This demo's own first pass did exactly this: at a 320px viewport the chart rendered at 280×146px for 30 months × 5 series, bars a few pixels wide. Fixed here via a horizontal-scroll floor around the chart's own legible size (see "Mobile / touch equivalent" below) — but it's a real, common gap worth checking for specifically, since a chart that "looks fine" on a desktop screenshot during an audit can fail this silently on mobile. (WCAG 1.4.10 Reflow)
  • A layered hit-target model can make one layer unreachable by pointer entirely — found in this very demo: each bar has a "total" hotspot stacked underneath its per-segment hotspots, but the segments' combined hit areas exactly cover the total hotspot's own area, so a mouse click or a touch tap anywhere on a bar always lands on a segment, never the total (confirmed via elementFromPoint at multiple points inside the total hotspot's own bounding box — a segment button was topmost every time). Keyboard and screen reader users don't hit this, since the total is still its own explicit stop in the roving-tabindex sequence; mouse/touch users had no equivalent at all until each segment's own text was extended to include the month's actual total, not just its percentage share (see the Segment stop row in "Expected accessibility tree" above). Worth checking for specifically whenever a chart nests more than one level of interactive detail in the same visual space. (WCAG 2.1.1 Keyboard is not violated here since keyboard access exists — this is a pointer-specific gap with no WCAG success criterion of its own, just a real usability failure.)
  • An interactive legend that only changes color, not keyboard/SR reachability — a common shortcut: dim a series' color when its legend entry is clicked, but leave its segment's hotspot/DOM node exactly where it was. A sighted mouse user sees it "hidden"; a keyboard or screen reader user still lands on a stop describing a segment that, visually, no longer exists — confusing at best, actively wrong if the announced percentage/total still assumes it's part of the stack. This demo's legend toggle actually removes the segment's hotspot from the DOM entirely when hidden (see "Legend as a filter" above), so keyboard nav has nothing stale to land on and every other segment's own total updates to match what's actually shown. (WCAG 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value — the state a sighted user sees and the state AT exposes must match)
  • Hover/focus tooltips left running on touch, unmodified — found on this very demo's own toolbar: tapping a plain toggle button left its tooltip stuck visible (no "leave" event ever fires on touch to hide it); tapping a button that opens a panel produced a visible flash (the tooltip shows on focus, then the panel-open code immediately hides it, both in one tap); tapping a button that navigates away (a CSV download) showed no tooltip at all (the page moves on before the focus-triggered tooltip renders). Three different symptoms from the same root cause: hover and focus are two separate signals on desktop, but tap collapses them into one ambiguous gesture with no reliable way to detect "the user has moved on." Fixed here by suppressing the tooltip outright under (hover: none), (pointer: coarse) (see "Mobile / touch equivalent" below) rather than patching each symptom separately — worth checking for specifically any time a tooltip mechanism built and tested with a mouse gets shipped without a pass on an actual touch device. (WCAG 2.5.1 Pointer Gestures is not violated here — there's no gesture being required — but this is exactly the kind of desktop-only testing gap that produces real touch failures with no single WCAG criterion to catch them)

Mobile / touch equivalent

Below roughly 900px, the chart stops shrinking (30 months × 5 series has a real legibility floor -- see the "Common failure patterns" note on this above) and scrolls horizontally instead, at native touch-scroll/scrollbar behavior with no custom gesture handling. A gradient-plus-chevron indicator at whichever edge has more content is a sighted-only convenience (aria-hidden) signaling "there's more this way"; it carries no information a keyboard or screen reader user doesn't already have via the hotspot layer below, which reaches every bar regardless of scroll position.

The hotspot buttons work as normal tap targets for sighted touch users, and are real focusable elements so switch-access and other assistive input methods reach them. Two honest caveats:

  • Roving tabindex helps hardware-keyboard users, not swipe navigation. VoiceOver (iOS) and TalkBack swipe gestures walk the accessibility tree in document order regardless of tabindex, so a mobile screen reader user swiping through this chart still encounters all 180 stops (30 months × 6 stops) one at a time — the scale mitigation described in the keyboard pattern section doesn't carry over to touch. This is a real, unresolved cost of dense charts on mobile, not something this demo papers over.
  • No distinct visual "focused/selected" state beyond the browser's default focus ring, which is not generally shown for touch taps at all. The hover/focus tooltip is deliberately suppressed on touch entirely (see next) rather than attempting a broken touch equivalent, so a sighted touch user gets no extra visual confirmation that a bar was "selected" for exploration — the chart's own visible state (the focused segment's fill/pattern) is what's left to notice.
  • The hover/focus tooltip is suppressed outright below (hover: hover) and (pointer: fine), rather than left to run. On a touch device, one tap collapses "hover" and "click" into a single ambiguous gesture, which produced three different broken behaviors depending on what the tapped toolbar button actually does: tapping "Toggle patterns" left the tooltip stuck visible (nothing ever fires a "leave" event on touch to hide it); tapping "Open keyboard shortcuts" produced a visible flash (the tooltip shows on focus, then this demo's own code immediately hides it the instant the panel opens — both happen in the same tap); and tapping "Download CSV" showed no tooltip at all (the download interrupts the page before the focus-triggered tooltip has anything to render). Rather than patch each of those three differently, the tooltip doesn't try to run on touch at all — an honest "doesn't attempt this" beats three different half-working attempts, and it costs nothing for AT users on touch, who already get the accessible name directly on tap regardless.
